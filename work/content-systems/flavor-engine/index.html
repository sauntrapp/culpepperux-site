Sauntr · AI character design · Internal prototype

>The voice was right. The judgment was wrong.

Samuel Sauntr is the personality behind Sauntr, a swipe-based local discovery app. In an internal test, he made a claim about a real business that he had no standing to make, and he sounded exactly like himself doing it. This is how that test changed what I design for an AI character: not just how it speaks, but what it's allowed to say.

Product
Sauntr, a swipe-based local discovery app (iOS)
Role
Founder; product and content design lead. I wrote the voice, the specs and the behavioral rules. An engineer implemented the prototypes.
Status
Prototyped and tested internally. Neither version described here has been released.
Timeline
April–October 2026
01 — Designing Samuel

A friend with taste, not a recommendation engine.

Most discovery apps describe places the same way: a rating, a category, a summary of what everyone agrees on. I wanted Sauntr to have a point of view. Samuel is that point of view: the friend who notices the one detail worth knowing, and knows when there isn't one.

Meet Samuel

A sommelier, not a salesperson.

The friend who'd toss a bag in the back of his '89 SL500 and just go. He'll tell you the one thing worth knowing about a place, and he'd rather say nothing than flatter you.

Observantnot generic
Confidentnot pushy
Easygoingnot peppy
Honest about limitsnot overclaiming

If the data doesn't support a specific observation, he says nothing.

02 — The generative prototype

Find the one detail worth knowing. Or say nothing.

The first prototype gave Samuel one job: read what people had written about a venue, and surface the single most specific, personality-defining detail, in his voice, in a sentence or two.

Internal prototype · never released

The prompt was a contract, not just a voice instruction.

The prompt set three requirements, and only one of them was about voice.

  1. Sound like Samuel. A distinctive observation in his voice, the way a close friend would put it.
  2. Earn it. The material had to contain something specific or unexpected enough to support a real observation.
  3. Otherwise, return exactly NONE. Not a weaker sentence. A defined response the app could act on.

Blank is better than generic. The NONE path was a design decision from the start, not a fallback added later.

That's content design deciding application behavior: a word in the prompt that removes a component from the screen.

Pipeline as prototyped
1 · Input

Source material

What people had written about the venue enters the pipeline.

2 · Cache

Reuse first

If an observation already exists for this venue, use it. No new request.

3 · Model

Claude

Writes one short observation under the prompt's constraints, or returns NONE.

4 · Validator

Checks in code

Length, end punctuation, and a list of generic phrases ("great atmosphere").

5 · Output

Display or silence

Only a line that passes is ever shown.

PASSSamuel's observation appears on the card.
NONE · REJECTSamuel's observation is hidden. Nothing replaces it.
Built in the prototype
  • Generation through Anthropic's Claude API
  • A Supabase cache of generated observations, checked before any model call
  • One model request per venue, only when no reusable result existed
  • A deterministic validator after generation; failed lines weren't shown
Specified in the contract
  • The NONE instruction in the locked prompt
  • Hiding Samuel's component on NONE
  • max_tokens: 200

Why it was built this way: caching cut redundant requests and kept costs in check, the token cap kept replies short, and structural rules lived in code, where they're enforced every time instead of requested every time.

So the prototype wasn't an unconstrained model writing whatever it liked. The prompt held the voice. The validator caught generic and malformed lines. But neither was built to tell a compelling observation from an unsupported allegation.

03 — When the voice was right, but the answer was wrong

He sounded exactly like Samuel. That was the problem.

In an internal test, the prototype produced a confident statement about how a real business treated its customers. It was specific, it was grammatical, and it was squarely in Samuel's voice. It passed every quality check the prototype had. It was also an allegation nobody could stand behind.

What happened in testing · recreated

Specific, on voice, and unsupported.

The line reads like a friend's warning. Every check the prototype ran was about writing quality, so it passed all of them.

  • PASSSpecific, not generic
  • PASSIn Samuel's voice
  • PASSThe right length and shape
  • MISSINGIs this a claim he's allowed to make?
Proposed · the behavioral contract

The question comes before the phrasing.

The contract asks what kind of statement this is before it asks whether it's well written. A factual allegation about a business isn't something Samuel is authorized to make, however good the sentence is.

  • CLAIMFactual allegation about a third party
  • EVIDENCESecondhand, and nothing he can verify
  • PERMISSIONNot authorized for this kind of claim
  • RESULTSilence. Nothing is rewritten.
Illustrative · revised design

He talks about what he can actually see.

Samuel stops describing what strangers say about a place. When he speaks, it's about something he can support, like the choices the member is making right now. When he can't support anything, the card simply has no Samuel line.

  • OKGrounded in the member's own choices
  • OKNo claims about the business
  • OKSilence remains a valid answer

A sentence can pass every stylistic requirement and still fail the standard for responsible communication.

04 — The diagnosis

The checks measured style. Nothing measured standing.

The prototype asked whether an observation was specific, coherent and on voice. Those are the right questions for writing. They're the wrong questions for deciding what a character can say about the world. Four distinctions were missing.

Fine for SamuelOut of bounds
An impression

"Has the feel of a place where regulars linger."

vs
A factual assertion

"The staff ignore anyone they don't know."

An observation

Something he noticed, framed as what it is.

vs
An allegation

A charge about how someone behaves.

A supported inference

A conclusion the material actually backs up.

vs
An invented conclusion

A pattern or a "word is" that nothing establishes.

A compelling detail

Interesting enough to say.

vs
An appropriate thing to publish

Interesting and his to say.

Voice guidelines tell a character how to sound. They don't tell it what it knows.

05 — Designing the behavioral contract

Not "be nicer." Decide what he's authorized to say.

Telling Samuel to avoid negative language wouldn't have fixed it. A flattering claim he can't support ("the staff know every regular by name") is just as unearned. The fix was a contract: boundaries on what the character may know, infer and assert, settled before any sentence gets written.

1Evidence before expression

An observation needs enough support before the system considers how to phrase it.

2Permission to claim

Different kinds of statements need different levels of evidence. Some aren't his to make at all.

3Voice within boundaries

Staying in character never outranks being truthful and grounded.

4Silence is valid

When the evidence isn't there, saying nothing beats filler or an unsupported claim.

How a line earns its place
Step 1

A candidate observation

Something Samuel might say.

Step 2

What kind of claim is it?

An impression, a note about the member's own choices, or an assertion about someone else.

Step 3

Is there enough evidence for that kind?

The bar rises with the claim.

→ if not, silence
Step 4

Say it in his voice

Without stretching past what the evidence supports.

→ if it overreaches, silence

These principles shaped the next round of work: a version of Samuel that speaks only about what members do in the app, with checks in code before and after the model, and silence as the default. It was specified and prototyped. It hasn't been released.

06 — The decision and the takeaway

The prototype didn't ship. The lesson did.

The original prototype was never released. This test wasn't the only consideration in that decision, but it's the one that changed how I work. The problem was never sentence quality. It was the character's authority, and the relationship between evidence and expression.

Samuel's job, before

What other people say about this place.

→
Samuel's job, after

What your own choices say about your taste.

"Don't just design how an AI character speaks. Design what it's allowed to know, infer, and say."

That's the part of content design that shapes how a system behaves, not just the words it produces. The voice tells you who the character is. The contract decides what it's allowed to do with that voice.
